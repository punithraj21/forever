# Keeping a Trading Journal

> **In one line:** A trading journal (a written record of every trade you take) turns your experience into lessons, so you slowly get better instead of repeating the same mistakes.

## 🎯 What you'll learn
- Why a journal helps you improve faster than memory alone.
- Exactly what to write down after every trade.
- How to do a simple weekly review to spot patterns.
- Why you must grade your decision, not just the win or loss.

## 📘 Key concepts

### Why memory lies to you
Your brain remembers feelings, not facts. A journal keeps the facts.

- After a loss, you may think "I always lose." That is a feeling, not a fact.
- After a win, you may forget you broke your own rules and got lucky.
- Like a shopkeeper who writes every sale in a book, you need records, not guesses.
- The journal shows the truth your memory hides.

### What to record for every trade
Write these down for each trade. Keep it short. A notebook, a spreadsheet, or an app all work.

- **Date and time** — when you entered.
- **Instrument** (the stock or item you traded, like Reliance or Infosys).
- **Setup / reason** (the signal that made you enter — your "why").
- **Entry price** — the price you bought or sold at.
- **Stop** (stop-loss: the price where you exit to limit loss).
- **Target** — the price where you planned to book profit.
- **Position size** — how many shares, and how much money at risk.
- **Screenshot of the chart** — a picture of the setup for later study.
- **Result** — profit or loss in money, and in **R** (R = your risk on that trade; if you risked ₹500 and made ₹1,000, that is +2R).
- **How you felt** — calm, scared, greedy, bored.
- **Did you follow your plan?** — a simple Yes or No.

### Grade the decision, not the outcome
This is the most important habit. A good decision can still lose. A bad decision can still win.

- Ask: "Did I follow my rules?" not "Did I make money?"
- A trade where you followed your plan but lost = a **good** trade.
- A trade where you broke your rules but won = a **bad** trade (you got lucky).
- Like a cricket batsman: playing the right shot is good even if you get out. Chasing a wild ball is bad even if you score.

## 🔍 Example

A beginner writes one row after a trade:

```
Date    | Stock    | Reason      | Entry | Stop | Target | Size | Result | R   | Felt  | Plan?
19 Sep  | Infosys  | Breakout    | 1500  | 1470 | 1560   | 10   | +₹600  | +2R | Calm  | Yes
```

- Risk was ₹30 per share × 10 = ₹300.
- Profit was ₹60 per share × 10 = ₹600.
- ₹600 ÷ ₹300 = **+2R**. A clean, planned win.

At the weekly review, they see 8 trades. All "Breakout" setups in the morning made money. All afternoon "tip from a friend" trades lost, and "Plan? = No" on every one. Now the lesson is clear: trade breakouts in the morning, stop taking tips.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Only writing down wins. You learn most from losses.
- Recording money but not the reason or feeling.
- Skipping the journal on busy days — then the record has holes.
- Judging yourself by profit alone, so bad habits that "won" survive.
- Never reviewing. Writing without reviewing is like buying groceries and never cooking.

## ✅ Key takeaways
- A journal turns random experience into real skill.
- Record the reason, the risk, the result in R, and your feelings.
- Do a short weekly review to find your best and worst patterns.
- Grade the decision (did you follow the plan?), not just the outcome.
- Honesty is everything — the journal only helps if it is true.

## 📝 Quick check
1. **Q:** You followed all your rules but the trade lost money. Was it a good or bad trade?
   **A:** A good trade. You made a good decision; losing is normal even with good decisions.
2. **Q:** What does "+2R" mean if you risked ₹500?
   **A:** You made two times your risk, so a ₹1,000 profit.
3. **Q:** Why do a weekly review instead of just writing trades down?
   **A:** The review reveals patterns — which setups and times make or lose money — so you can repeat what works and drop what does not.

## 📖 New words
- **Trading journal** — a written record of every trade you take, used to learn and improve.
- **Instrument** — the thing you trade, such as a share of a company.
- **Setup** — the signal or pattern that gives you a reason to enter a trade.
- **Stop / stop-loss** — the price where you exit to limit your loss.
- **Target** — the price where you plan to take your profit.
- **Position size** — how many shares you buy, and how much money is at risk.
- **R** — your risk on one trade, used as a unit; +2R means you gained twice your risk.

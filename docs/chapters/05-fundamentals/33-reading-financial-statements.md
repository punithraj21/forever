# Reading Financial Statements

> **In one line:** Every company keeps three report cards, and once you know how to read them, you can tell a strong shop from a shaky one.

## 🎯 What you'll learn
- What the three main financial statements are.
- What the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow each tell you.
- The simple rule that keeps a balance sheet balanced.
- What a beginner should look for before buying a share.

## 📘 Key concepts

Think of a company like a shop in a busy bazaar. To know if the shop is doing well, you would want to see three things: how much it earned, what it owns and owes, and how much real cash moved. That is exactly what the three statements show.

### The income statement (Profit & Loss, or P&L)
This shows money earned and spent over a **period** (a stretch of time, like 3 months or 1 year).

- **Revenue** (also called sales) — the total money the shop took in from customers.
- **Expenses** — the money it spent to run the shop (rent, salaries, raw material).
- **Net income** (also called profit) — what is left after expenses. Revenue minus expenses.
- **EPS** (earnings per share) — profit divided by the number of shares. It tells you how much profit belongs to one single share.
- Analogy: your salary slip. Salary minus your spending equals what you save that month.

### The balance sheet
This is a **snapshot** (a photo at one exact moment, not over time). It shows what the company owns and owes on that day.

- **Assets** — things the company owns (cash, land, machines, stock of goods).
- **Liabilities** — things the company owes (loans, unpaid bills).
- **Shareholders' equity** — what is left for the owners after paying off everything owed.
- The golden rule: **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**. It must always balance, like a see-saw.
- Analogy: your personal net worth. Your house and savings (own) minus your loans (owe) equals your true worth.

### The cash flow statement
Profit on paper can be dressed up. **Cash** (actual money in the bank) is much harder to fake. So this statement is a truth-check.

- **Operating cash flow** — cash from the main business (selling goods). This is the most important one.
- **Investing cash flow** — cash used to buy or sell big things like machines.
- **Financing cash flow** — cash from taking loans, repaying loans, or paying dividends.
- Analogy: your bank passbook. It shows real money coming in and going out, not just promises.

## 🔍 Example

Meet "Chai Corner Ltd", a tea-stall company. Here are its numbers for one year:

- Revenue (sales): ₹100
- Expenses: ₹80
- Net income (profit): ₹100 − ₹80 = **₹20**
- Shares: 10, so EPS = ₹20 ÷ 10 = **₹2 per share**

Its balance sheet on the last day:

```
Assets ₹150  =  Liabilities ₹60  +  Equity ₹90
```

The see-saw balances: 60 + 90 = 150. Good.

Its cash flow shows **₹18 of operating cash flow** — close to the ₹20 profit. That means the profit is real cash, not just an entry on paper. A healthy sign.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Looking only at profit and ignoring cash flow. Paper profit with no cash is a red flag.
- Judging a company from one year alone. Always compare 3 to 5 years.
- Ignoring debt. High loans (liabilities) can sink even a profitable shop.
- Chasing a big revenue number while the company still makes a loss.
- Forgetting that revenue is not profit. A shop can sell a lot and still lose money.

## ✅ Key takeaways
- Three statements: income statement, balance sheet, cash flow.
- Income statement = profit over time (Revenue − Expenses = Profit).
- Balance sheet = a snapshot; Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
- Cash flow = real money movement, the honesty check.
- Look for growing revenue and profit, positive operating cash flow, and debt that is not too high.

## 📝 Quick check
1. **Q:** Which statement is a snapshot at one moment in time?
   **A:** The balance sheet.
2. **Q:** What does the rule "Assets = Liabilities + Equity" mean?
   **A:** What a company owns equals what it owes plus what belongs to the owners; it must always balance.
3. **Q:** Why is cash flow useful even when a company shows a profit?
   **A:** Cash is harder to fake than paper profit, so it checks that the profit is real money.

## 📖 New words
- **Revenue (sales)** — total money a company takes in from customers.
- **Expenses** — money spent to run the business.
- **Net income (profit)** — what is left after expenses are taken out of revenue.
- **EPS (earnings per share)** — profit divided by the number of shares.
- **Assets** — things a company owns.
- **Liabilities** — things a company owes, like loans.
- **Shareholders' equity** — the owners' share after all debts are paid.
- **Operating cash flow** — real cash earned from the main business.
- **Period** — a stretch of time a statement covers, like a year.
- **Snapshot** — a picture of one exact moment in time.
